The problem

Why gratings at all

A neutron travelling through matter sees a refractive index that is almost exactly one:

n = 1 − δ,   δ = λ2 N bc / 2π ≈ 10−6 For aluminium at 4 Å, δ ≈ 5 × 10−6. N bc is the coherent scattering length density.

A gradient of that index across the beam tilts the wavefront by an angle α of a few micro-radians. Over one metre that is a lateral displacement of a few micrometres, far below what an imaging detector with 50 to 100 µm pixels can see. Three things stand in the way of measuring it directly:

  • The effect is tiny. You need a ruler with micrometre graduations. A grating with a period of a few µm is that ruler.
  • Neutron sources are incoherent and dim. Classical interferometry needs a source that is coherent across the whole instrument. A grating interferometer only needs coherence across one grating period.
  • Detectors are coarse. Nobody can resolve 4 µm fringes with 50 µm pixels. A second grating converts the fringe position into a plain intensity, which any detector reads.

Every design on this page is a different answer to those three constraints. They all share one building block: the Talbot self-imaging effect.

Building block

The Talbot effect

Illuminate a periodic grating with a (locally) plane wave and the field behind it is not a shadow. Diffraction orders interfere, and at well defined distances the grating reproduces an image of itself. For a grating of period p and wavelength λ the pattern repeats with the Talbot length

zT = 2 p2 / λ For p = 7.97 µm and λ = 4.1 Å, zT = 310 mm.

Between repeats the pattern goes through fractional self-images: shifted copies, copies with half the period, and (for a phase grating) places where a pure phase modulation turns into a pure intensity modulation. That last conversion is the whole trick: a phase grating absorbs nothing, yet a few centimetres downstream it produces a high-contrast intensity pattern. Explore the "Talbot carpet" below. Propagation distance runs left to right, the lateral position across three grating periods runs top to bottom.

Three things to notice:

  • Absorption grating. It is already an intensity pattern at z = 0, and it reappears at zT. At zT/4 and 3zT/4 the pattern has half the period. It throws away half the beam before doing anything useful, so it is not used as the first grating.
  • π/2 phase grating. Invisible at z = 0 (phase only), full contrast with the original period p at zT/4 and 3zT/4.
  • π phase grating. Contrast appears with period p/2 at zT/16, 3zT/16, 5zT/16 and so on. The first of these, d1 = p2/8λ, is only 19 mm for the example grating, which keeps the instrument compact. Most neutron Talbot-Lau setups use a π grating for that reason.

Note the λ-dependence. A phase grating etched to give π at 4.1 Å gives π/2 at 2 Å, and the distance of every self-image scales as 1/λ. On a time-of-flight instrument such as VENUS, each wavelength frame therefore sees a different point of this carpet. The fringe visibility oscillates with wavelength, and the grating design must pick the band to optimise.

2 gratings · G1 + G2

The Talbot interferometer

The simplest instrument places one phase grating G1 in a coherent beam and an absorption grating G2 at the first fractional Talbot distance d. A sample in front of G1 refracts the beam by α, so the self-image behind G1 slides sideways by d·α. G2, whose period matches the self-image, converts that slide into an intensity change the detector can read.

Why the analyser grating is needed, and what "phase stepping" means

A detector pixel is 10 to 25 times wider than the self-image period, so on its own it averages the fringes to a flat grey. G2 is a mask with the same period as the self-image. When its bars sit on the dark fringes, the bright fringes pass; slide it by half a period and the bars block them. Moving G2 in small steps across one period and recording the pixel value at each step traces a sinusoid. Everything the interferometer measures is a property of that sinusoid.

The catch. The coherence condition λL/s ≥ p1 at 4 Å and L = 5 m demands a slit of about 0.2 mm, which starves the detector. Neutron imaging beams normally use apertures of 10 to 20 mm, so with two gratings alone the fringes wash out. The third grating fixes that.

3 gratings · G0 + G1 + G2

The Talbot-Lau interferometer

Put a coarse absorption grating G0 right after a large, incoherent source. Each slit of G0 is narrow enough to be its own coherent line source, so each slit produces its own sharp self-image at G2. The slits are mutually incoherent, so their self-images simply add as intensities. Lau's observation (1948) is that if the slit pitch p0 is chosen so that neighbouring self-images are displaced by exactly one period p2, they add in register and the contrast survives. The source can then be as large as you like.

p0 = p2 · L / d Shift of the self-image per slit pitch: p0·d/L. Setting that equal to p2 is the Lau condition.

Formulas: dn = n p12/8λ for a π grating (p2 = p1/2), dn = n p12/2λ for π/2 (p2 = p1), both multiplied by (L+d)/L for the diverging beam. Silicon depth h = φ/(λ N bc) with N bc(Si) = 2.07 × 10−6 Å−2. ξ = λd/p2.

Numbers worth remembering for neutrons: the analyser G2 and the source grating G0 are absorption gratings, usually gadolinium about 10 to 30 µm thick with a period of a few µm (G2) or about a millimetre (G0). G1 is a silicon phase grating a few tens of µm deep. Each absorption grating throws away half the beam, so a Talbot-Lau instrument transmits at most about a quarter of the incoming neutrons before the sample.

Analysis

Reading the three signals

Phase stepping is done twice: once with the sample out (reference) and once with it in. For every pixel both scans are fitted with

I(xg) = a0 + a1 cos(2π xg/p2 + φ)

and the three images are ratios and differences of the fit parameters:

T = a0s / a0r     Δφ = φs − φr     DFI = Vs / Vr,   V = a1/a0 s = sample scan, r = reference scan, V = visibility.

Move the sliders to see how each sample property reshapes the stepping curve. The sampled points are what the detector records at eight grating positions; the fit recovers the curve.

Reference visibility set to 30 %, typical for neutron Talbot-Lau. The refraction angle uses p2 = 4 µm and d = 19.4 mm. In practice Δφ wraps at ±π, so strongly refracting edges need phase unwrapping.

The differential phase is called differential because the interferometer is sensitive to the gradient of the phase across the grating lines, not to the phase itself. Integrating the DPC image along that direction recovers the phase, hence the thickness-weighted N bc of the sample. The dark-field image has no counterpart in conventional radiography; it is what the next sections are about.

2 phase gratings · no analyser

The far-field interferometer

The Talbot designs pay twice for their micrometre fringes: an absorbing analyser throws away half the beam, and the analyser has to be stepped. Miao and colleagues (2016, with X-rays) and Pushin, Sarenac, Hussey and colleagues (2017, with neutrons) showed a way around both. Two phase gratings with the same period p are placed a small distance D apart in a diverging beam, far from the detector. Each grating's pattern is projected onto the detector with a slightly different magnification, and the two nearly-equal periods beat against each other. The beat is a moiré fringe with a period of millimetres, which an ordinary imaging detector resolves directly:

P = p · L / D L is the total source to detector distance. With p = 2.4 µm, L = 4 m and D = 10 mm, P ≈ 1 mm.

Nothing absorbs, there is no Talbot distance to respect, and the fringe period does not depend on wavelength, so a polychromatic beam keeps its fringes (only the visibility varies with λ, since the phase shift does). The whole interferometer is a few centimetres long and can be placed anywhere in the beam.

ξ = λ L2 / P = λ L2 D / (p L). Moving the gratings a few millimetres apart changes ξ by an order of magnitude without touching the wavelength or the sample. That is the far-field design's main selling point for dark-field work.

Variants exist. A third phase grating in the middle (Sarenac et al. 2018) doubles the fringe period for the same D and relaxes the source coherence, so a larger slit can be used. A source grating G0 can be added in front, exactly as in the Talbot-Lau case, when the beam is too incoherent for the slit. And since the moiré period is set by geometry alone, these instruments work well in white or pulsed beams.

Interpretation

Dark-field is small-angle scattering in real space

Why does scattering reduce the fringe visibility? A grating interferometer compares the wave at two points separated by a fixed transverse distance, the autocorrelation length ξ. Small-angle scattering by structures in the sample randomises the phase between those two points, and the fringe contrast falls. Strobl (2014) showed that for a sample of thickness t with macroscopic scattering cross-section Σ the dark-field signal is

DFI(ξ) = exp[ −Σ t (1 − G(ξ)) ],   ξ = λ Ls / p2 G(ξ) is the projected real-space correlation function of the structure. Ls is the sample to analyser distance (Talbot-Lau) or the sample to detector distance divided by P/p2 equivalents (far-field, see the box above).

That is the same correlation function spin-echo SANS measures. For monodisperse spheres of diameter Ds it is known in closed form (Andersson et al. 2008): G = 1 at ξ = 0 and falls to zero at ξ = Ds. So DFI(ξ) starts at 1, drops, and levels off at exp(−Σt) once ξ exceeds the structure size. The knee gives the size; the plateau gives the amount of scattering material.

The two brackets under the axis show which ξ each instrument reaches. Talbot-Lau: p2 = 4 µm, Ls = 19.4 mm, λ swept from 2 to 8 Å by time of flight. Far-field: p = 2.4 µm, L = 4 m, L2 = 3 m, λ = 4.4 Å, D swept from 1 to 50 mm.

Three ways to scan ξ, one per instrument type:

  • Talbot-Lau at a pulsed source. ξ is proportional to λ, so a time-of-flight measurement delivers a whole ξ scan in one exposure. The grating is designed for one wavelength, so the reference visibility varies across the band and must be measured per wavelength bin.
  • Talbot-Lau, sample position. Moving the sample along the beam changes Ls, hence ξ, at fixed λ. Limited by the space between gratings.
  • Far-field. Change the grating spacing D. Two orders of magnitude in ξ with a translation stage, at a fixed wavelength and a fixed sample position.

Dark-field imaging with neutrons has been used for magnetic domain walls (they refract the two spin states in opposite directions), porosity in ceramics and additively manufactured metals, cracks, powders and fibre orientation in composites. Anything with structure between about 100 nm and 10 µm that is invisible in transmission.
